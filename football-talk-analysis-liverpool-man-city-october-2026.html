Liverpool v Man City: the red card changed the match, but not the whole story

Liverpool’s 0–3 defeat at Anfield split into two very different phases. The numbers make the dismissal’s impact clear; they also leave an important question about what Liverpool can learn from the hour before it.

Football Talk Editorial · Published 13 October 2026 · Match analysis

For an hour, this looked like a contest Liverpool could still shape. The score remained 0–0, and the Premier League’s match review described the game as close, with Liverpool arguably on top in the second half. Then Ryan Gravenberch was sent off in the 62nd minute after two bookings. Manchester City scored 2 minutes and 37 seconds later and went on to win 3–0.

That sequence is the obvious turning point. It is also where a simple post-match explanation can stop being useful. The dismissal helps explain the scale and timing of the defeat. It cannot, on its own, explain why the match stayed level for an hour, or what Liverpool should take from the period when both teams had eleven players.

What the published evidence says

  • The first goal came shortly after Gravenberch’s 62nd-minute dismissal.
  • City recorded 11 of their 15 shots while Liverpool were down to ten players.
  • Jeremy Doku played 34 minutes, made five dribbles, created two chances and took two shots.
  • Rayan Cherki’s third goal completed a 41-pass move involving every City player.

These figures are from the Premier League’s matchweek review.

The red card explains the second phase

Eleven of fifteen City shots came after Liverpool went a player down: roughly 73 per cent. That is strong evidence that the dismissal changed the match’s attacking balance. City found more room, and the opening goal arrived before Liverpool could reorganise. From that point, protecting the middle and covering the spaces around the ball became harder.

Doku’s impact gives the change a human shape. In only 34 minutes he led City for dribbles and contributed directly to the first goal before scoring himself. A fresh, direct winger against a tiring ten-player side is a difficult problem. The numbers support the view that City exploited the advantage well; they do not prove that the result was inevitable.

The hour Liverpool need to review

The more revealing part for Liverpool is the scoreless hour. If the team were competitive—and the match review says Liverpool arguably had the better of the second half before the dismissal—then this was not simply a case of City dominating from the first whistle. Liverpool will want to understand which moments kept the game close and whether they were creating enough to turn that position into a lead.

That distinction matters for the next match. “The red card cost us” describes the turning point, but it offers little preparation for a similar contest. Reviewing the eleven-against-eleven phase asks a more useful set of questions: where did Liverpool win territory, what did City concede, and did Liverpool turn promising possession into clear chances? The available shot split does not answer those questions on its own, so we should not pretend that it does.

Football Talk verdict: City’s three goals should be read in two parts. The dismissal was the decisive change in conditions, and City’s use of the extra player was clinical. Liverpool’s next step is to learn from the competitive phase before it—not to let the red card erase it from the review.

What to watch next

Watch whether Liverpool can keep creating threat when matches are level, and whether the side can respond more quickly when a game changes shape. For City, the questions are different: how often can Doku provide this kind of one-against-one threat, and can the team keep turning patient possession into chances against eleven players too? One result offers clues, not a season-long conclusion.
