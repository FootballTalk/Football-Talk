If a Premier League title is removed, should the runner-up inherit it?

Football Talk’s view is that a title removed after the season should be declared null and void. The runner-up should not automatically be crowned champion.

Football Talk Editorial · Published 4 October 2026 · Opinion

The question of what happens to a title after a serious rules breach is not just about moving a trophy from one cabinet to another. It is about what the competition’s result means, how a sanction should repair harm, and whether a club that finished second should be declared champion years later.

That question is now being discussed around Manchester City’s Premier League case. On 29 September, the League said an independent Commission had found City guilty of the financial-rule charges relating to the 2009/10 to 2017/18 seasons, along with three of four groups of allegations concerning co-operation. City appealed on 2 October. The sanction stage is separate and no sanction has yet been announced. The appeal process is also still underway.

Those are the published procedural facts. What the eventual sporting outcome should be is a matter of debate. Football Talk’s position is clear: if a Premier League title is taken away after the season, the title should be left vacant rather than awarded to the club that finished second.

A sanction must fit the breach

There is a strong argument for serious consequences when a club is found to have gained an unfair advantage. A sanction needs to protect the competition, deter future breaches and recognise the effect on other clubs. A fine alone may not achieve that if the breach changed the competitive picture.

But deciding that a champion should lose a title does not automatically answer who should receive it. The runner-up completed that season under the rules and results as they stood at the time. A later ruling may establish that the competition was compromised; it does not replay the matches or show exactly where every other club would have finished under different circumstances.

That does not erase the runner-up’s achievement. It means that finishing second is not the same as winning a title that was never awarded to you on the pitch. If a formal sanction removes the champion from the record, declaring the title vacant is a cleaner statement of what happened: the competition was completed, but no champion is recognised for that season.

Why automatic redistribution creates another dispute

Handing the title to the next club in the table sounds simple, but it creates a new sporting judgement. Should the second-placed club inherit the championship automatically, even if the underlying breach affected the whole league? What about clubs whose European qualification, prize money or relegation position was also shaped by the same table? Should those outcomes be recalculated too?

There may be cases where rules explicitly require a particular remedy. That is why any decision has to follow the competition regulations and the independent process, rather than a crowd vote or a rival club’s preference. The Premier League says its Commission has broad discretion over sanctions, including fines, points deductions and other sporting sanctions. Its statement also says parties can appeal, and the Appeal Board has discretion to vary the Commission’s order.

Those powers are broad, but the League has not announced the sanction in this case. It would be wrong to present title removal or redistribution as a settled outcome. The appeal must be resolved and the sanction process completed before supporters can assess what has actually been ordered.

Football Talk’s position
If the eventual sanction removes a Premier League title, leave that season without an officially recognised champion. Do not transfer the honour automatically to the runner-up. Apply any further sporting sanction under the rules and explain clearly how it protects the integrity of the competition.

Fairness means being consistent

Consistency matters as much as severity. Clubs and supporters need to know that comparable breaches are judged through the same rules, with reasons published and a genuine right of appeal. A remedy should not be designed to make one rival happy, and it should not assume that every table position can be rewritten without consequences.

Leaving a title vacant is not a claim that the runner-up did nothing to earn their finish. It is a refusal to rewrite the history of a season by appointing a new champion who did not win it at the time. If the independent process concludes that the title cannot stand, the record should say exactly that: no champion.

Have your say

Should a stripped Premier League title be left vacant, or should it go to the runner-up? Is a points deduction a fairer response than changing the historical record? Football Talk wants supporters to debate the principle while the case continues, without treating an unresolved sanction as a foregone conclusion.
